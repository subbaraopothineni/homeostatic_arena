Behavioural markers are increasingly proposed as evidence of sentience or self-awareness in non-human systems, including artificial agents. A marker is informative only if simpler mechanisms cannot produce it. We tested four animal-sentience markers and a prediction-based test of self/other distinction in a physics-simulated agent whose only learning signal is the reduction of homeostatic drive. In Study 1, a memoryless thermotactic reflex passed anticipatory avoidance perfectly, leaving every heat encounter before its nociceptor fired, whereas no reflex passed the motivational trade-off or sensitization markers; the usual sensitization metric also carried a built-in positive bias of about 9 °C in memoryless agents. In Study 2 (pre-registered), we asked whether recurrent agents trained with a self-supervised forward model predict self-caused sensory changes better than identical changes caused by another agent or by chance. The test was validated: a hand-built comparator passed, untrained networks did not, a position shortcut was exposed, and a post-hoc sensitivity analysis showed that anticipating 2% of a self-caused change would be detected. None of five trained agents supported any hypothesis; they had learned to let another agent operate the switch. Two further pre-registered studies, one in which acting was necessary and one applying the Study 1 markers to learners, were inconclusive because the agents did not acquire the relevant behaviour. Tests pre-registered as vulnerable were passed by trivial controls. Markers of sentience and self-awareness must therefore be benchmarked against minimal mechanisms, and in our hands the learning agents, not the tests, were the limiting factor.
