Behavioural markers are increasingly proposed as evidence of sentience in non-human systems, including artificial agents. A marker is informative only if simpler mechanisms cannot produce it. We tested four behavioural markers adapted from animal-sentience frameworks, and a comparator-based test of the sense of agency, in a physics-simulated agent whose only learning signal is the reduction of homeostatic drive. Every behavioural marker was met by a mechanism with at most one bit of memory. A memoryless thermotactic reflex left every heat encounter before its nociceptor fired. A reflex whose withdrawal is vetoed by critically low energy accepted a noxious route only when in need. A reflex with one bit of state became more cautious after harm (−3.2 °C). A standard trade-off score missed that genuine trade-off because need changed within a trial, and a standard sensitization metric was biased by about +9 °C. A pre-registered study asked whether recurrent agents trained with a self-supervised forward model predict self-caused sensory changes better than identical changes with other causes. Positive, negative and shortcut controls validated the test, which detected a self-model contributing 2–25% of the prediction, depending on its timing precision. No trained agent supported any hypothesis: their forward models did not predict the relevant channels at all, and they had learned to let another agent operate the switch. Two further pre-registered studies were inconclusive: the learners never acquired the relevant behaviour. Behavioural markers of sentience must be benchmarked against minimal mechanisms; prediction-based agency tests can be made valid but require competent learners.
